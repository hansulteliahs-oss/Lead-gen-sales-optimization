# Product

## Register

brand

## Users

The website's primary visitors are **dual-income families in Southern California (Newport Beach, Costa Mesa, Huntington Beach, Irvine, Laguna Beach, and the broader OC/SD corridor) with young children, where both parents work and current childcare is straining the budget.** Secondary: single working parents in the same area weighing options.

**Their context when they arrive:**
- Already paying for some form of childcare (daycare, after-school, occasional nanny) and feeling the cost.
- Researching alternatives. The au pair program is unfamiliar to most; they're trying to figure out what it is, what it costs, whether it's safe, and whether it fits their family.
- Often researching late at night after the kids are in bed. Tired. Skimming, not reading.
- Comparing Kim's site against generic Cultural Care corporate pages, other au pair agencies, and traditional childcare sites.

**The job they're trying to get done:**
1. Quickly understand what an au pair is and roughly what it costs versus what they pay now.
2. Decide whether to trust *this person* (Kim) to guide them through it.
3. Take the next step, click out to the Cultural Care signup form, with confidence that someone real is on the other side.

The site succeeds when a parent thinks: *"This is a real person, this seems doable, I'd talk to her."*

## Product Purpose

A personal-brand marketing site for an individual Local Childcare Consultant (currently Kim Arvdalen, with the platform built to support more LCCs over time) that turns a generic-feeling Cultural Care program into a personal recommendation from a trusted local guide.

The site's job is to:
- Replace the cold, corporate Cultural Care default experience with a warm, personal one.
- Educate visitors enough that the au pair program feels concrete, safe, and affordable.
- Hand off to the Cultural Care signup form with the visitor primed to engage with Kim specifically.

Success looks like: more qualified families clicking through to the Cultural Care form because they trust Kim, not the program. The site is a relationship starter, not a transaction.

## Brand Personality

**Warm, trustworthy, neighborly, easy to read.**

- **Warm:** Like a friend who happens to be the expert. The opposite of a sales page.
- **Trustworthy:** A real person, real photos, real area served, real testimonials. No stock-photo families. No vague platitudes.
- **Neighborly:** Of this place (Newport Beach, OC, SoCal), not generic-California. Specific street names, towns, beaches, schools where it's natural. Premium because it's local, not because it's polished.
- **Easy to read:** A tired parent at 10pm should be able to skim the page and walk away with the answer. Information density beats decoration. Headlines do work; subheads do work; body copy is short.

Voice: first-person from Kim where it makes sense. Conversational, never breezy. Kind, never saccharine.

## Anti-references

What this should explicitly **not** look or feel like:

- **Corporate Cultural Care HQ pages.** Stock photography, generic gradients, "trusted by families" trust-bar treatments. We are the personal-brand counterweight to those execution patterns, not a regional copy of the corporate template. A single restrained magenta accent is the only deliberate visual link to the Cultural Care brand; the rest of the system stays editorial-warm.
- **Generic Wix/Squarespace au pair templates.** Cookie-cutter consultant sites with the same hero + 3-card services + testimonial slider + footer.
- **Pinterest-mom / Etsy-shop kitsch.** Script fonts, watercolor flourishes, oversaturated pastel pinks, "Hey Mama!" copy. Warm is not the same as cute. (The editorial magenta accent is bold and restrained, not pastel — this rule still has teeth.)
- **Tech-startup SaaS landing pages.** Hero metric stat blocks, gradient buttons, identical icon-and-heading card grids, "Why families love us" feature lists. We are not a SaaS product.
- **Over-the-top portrait worship.** Every section anchored by a giant photo of Kim. She's a person, not a brand mascot. Her presence should feel earned, not omnipresent.

## Design Principles

These are the strategic rules that decide trade-offs when designing or auditing any page.

1. **A real person, not a logo.** Every page should feel like it was made for Kim specifically, by Kim or someone who knows her. If a section could be lifted onto another LCC's site by changing only the name, it's too generic. The platform supports many LCCs; the *output* has to feel one-of-one.

2. **Information beats decoration.** A tired parent skimming at 10pm has to walk away with the answer. If a design choice (a flourish, an animation, a layout twist) makes the page prettier but harder to scan, the design loses. Beauty must serve clarity.

3. **Address the cost question directly.** These visitors arrived because childcare is expensive. Pretending money doesn't matter is condescending. Cost, comparison, and value live near the surface, not buried.

4. **Warm without softening expertise.** Warmth is the entry point; competence is what closes. Kim is the local guide who has done this hundreds of times. The site should feel like a kitchen-table conversation with someone who knows exactly what to do, not a brochure.

5. **Refuse the category template.** The au-pair / family-services category has a default look (pastel pink, rounded cards, smiling stock families). When in doubt, do the opposite of the category and the site gets stronger. We are a personal-brand editorial site that happens to market the au pair program. A single restrained magenta accent acknowledges the program we hand off to; the rest of the system stays warm-editorial, not corporate-affiliate.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

- **WCAG 2.1 AA** as the floor. Color contrast, focus visibility, semantic landmarks, alt text on every meaningful image.
- **Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.** Fade-in scroll sections must not trigger for users who have requested reduced motion.
- **Keyboard navigability** for the entire site, including the mobile nav menu.
- **Screen reader sanity:** the LCC name in the nav links to home; "Get Started" CTAs have descriptive accessible names (not five separate "Get Started" buttons indistinguishable on a screen reader).
- **Tired-parent inclusion:** functional accessibility for users who are exhausted, scanning on a phone, single-handed (one thumb on the screen, one hand holding a kid). This is a UX constraint as much as an a11y one.
- No specific user accommodations called out yet, but design must not assume perfect vision, perfect attention, or perfect motor control.
